    Strategy and procurement view

    Prototype — decision-maker persona

    This version is for people who make decisions about planning technology without designing the integrations themselves.

    It aims to help digital leaders, procurement professionals, service managers and heads of planning ask better questions and avoid common pitfalls when changing planning systems.

    What this page is

    Planning case management systems do not work alone. They rely on other things your council provides — and when you change your planning system, those relationships change too.

    This page explains:

    • what your planning system actually depends on;
    • why that matters when you procure, replace or change it;
    • what questions to ask — of suppliers, and of your own colleagues; and
    • how to think about what should be "inside" the planning product and what should be provided by other council services.

    It does not tell you what to buy, who to buy it from, or what your council should do. Those decisions depend on your context. It aims to help you ask the right questions before you commit.

    What planning technology depends on

    A planning case management system handles the core work — receiving applications, tracking them through assessment, and recording decisions. But to do that, it needs several other capabilities: To validate

    Storing documents

    Every application comes with documents — plans, reports, photos, consultation responses, decision notices. These need to be stored, found again, and in many cases made public. Planning generates far more documents than most council services — often hundreds per application.

    Taking payments

    Applications require fees. Someone needs to calculate the fee (which depends on what's being proposed), take the payment, and confirm it was received before the case can progress.

    Sending notifications

    The council must send acknowledgements, consultation notices, requests for information and decisions. Many of these are legally required within specific timeframes — late notifications can create legal risk.

    Knowing where things are

    Every application relates to a specific site. The system needs to connect that site to maps, addresses, and the constraints that apply (conservation areas, flood zones, tree preservation orders). In planning, location is not just a reference — it determines which rules apply.

    Controlling access

    Different people need to see different things. Applicants see their own case. Officers see everything. The public sees what's on the register. Consultees see the parts they're asked to comment on. Managing who sees what is essential.

    Keeping the formal record

    Planning decisions are permanent legal records. The council must maintain a public register and keep case records accessible long after the case is closed. This is different from normal document retention — planning records don't get deleted after 7 years.

    The key point: Your current planning system probably provides all of these itself — bundled inside one product. Your council may also provide some of them separately as corporate services (a shared payment system, a corporate document store, a mapping platform). The question is: which arrangement is better for your council?

    Why this matters when you change systems

    If your planning system provides all of these capabilities internally, replacing it means moving everything at once — all the documents, all the spatial data, all the payment history, all the case records. This is widely reported to cost over £1 million per council and take 12-18 months.

    The risk you may not see

    When everything lives inside one product, you are not just buying a case management system. You are buying document storage, a GIS viewer, a payment processor, a notification engine and a records archive — all tied to one supplier. If you later want to change any single component, you may find you cannot do so without replacing everything.

    Conversely, if you insist on separating everything, you may create integration complexity that your council cannot maintain — and the planning service suffers because systems don't reliably talk to each other.

    Neither "all bundled" nor "all separate" is automatically correct. The useful question is: for each capability, what arrangement gives your council the best balance of reliability, flexibility, cost and continuity?

    Questions to ask suppliers

    When evaluating a planning system, these questions help you understand the real scope of what you're buying and what happens if things change: To validate

    About scope and boundaries

    • Which of these capabilities does your product provide internally, and which does it expect the council to provide separately?
    • If we already have a corporate payment service (or DMS, or notification service), can your product use it instead of its own? What does that integration look like?
    • If we want to change one component in future (e.g. move documents to a corporate store), can we do that without replacing your entire product?

    About data and exit

    • If we decide to move to a different supplier, what data can we export? In what format?
    • Does exported data include the relationships between cases, documents, payments and spatial objects — or just flat tables?
    • Are documents stored in your system in open formats, or do they require your software to access?
    • What happens to our data if your company is acquired, changes its product, or ceases trading?

    About integration

    • Do you provide documented, supported APIs for each integration point? Are they open standards or proprietary?
    • If we need to connect your system to our corporate GIS, payment service, or notification platform, whose responsibility is building and maintaining that connection?
    • What happens to the planning service if an external integration is temporarily unavailable? Does work stop, or can it continue and reconcile later?

    Questions to ask colleagues

    Before deciding what the planning product should provide and what should be shared, establish what already exists in your council:

    Ask your IT or digital team

    • Do we have a corporate payment platform? Could planning use it? What would need to happen?
    • Do we have a corporate document management system? Could it handle planning's volume and public access requirements?
    • Do we have a corporate GIS platform? Does it hold the constraint data that planning needs?
    • Do we use GOV.UK Notify (or similar) for citizen communications? Could planning use it for statutory notifications?
    • If planning were to use these corporate services, who would be responsible for maintaining the connections?

    Ask your planning service

    • What does the current planning system actually do beyond case management? (You may find it provides maps, document storage, payment collection and public access — not just case tracking.)
    • If we separated any of those functions out, what would need to keep working exactly as it does today for the service to operate?
    • Where are the pain points with the current system? Are they about the case management itself, or about one of the bundled capabilities (e.g. poor search, clunky payments, unreliable notifications)?

    Ask your finance team

    • How are planning fees currently reconciled with our finance system?
    • If planning used our corporate payment platform, how would fees get coded and reconciled?
    • Who currently handles planning refunds, and how?

    Example: who provides the payment capability?

    To make this concrete, consider one capability — payments — and two possible arrangements:

    Scenario A: The planning supplier provides payments

    The planning product calculates the fee, takes the payment, and records it internally.

    Advantages: Simple — everything is in one place. No integration to maintain.

    Concerns:

    • Does this duplicate your corporate payment platform? Are you paying for the same capability twice?
    • Do citizens get a different payment experience for planning than for council tax or parking? Should they?
    • If you change planning supplier, can you get the payment records out? In what format?
    • How does the planning supplier's payment system reconcile with your finance system?

    Scenario B: The council's shared payment service handles it

    The planning product calculates the fee, then sends the citizen to the council's payment platform (e.g. GOV.UK Pay). The payment platform takes the money and tells planning it was successful.

    Advantages: Consistent citizen experience. No duplicate payment infrastructure. If you change planning supplier, the payment capability continues unchanged.

    Concerns:

    • Who is responsible for making the connection between planning and the payment service work? Who maintains it?
    • What happens if the payment service is temporarily unavailable — can the application still be submitted?
    • Who handles refunds? Does the planning officer initiate it, or does finance?
    • Can your corporate payment service handle planning's specific needs (e.g. complex fee calculation, partial payments, exemptions)?

    The point of this example is not to tell you which scenario is better — it depends on your council. The point is that "buy a planning system" is not a single decision. It contains within it a series of smaller decisions about which capabilities belong inside the product and which don't. Making those decisions consciously — rather than inheriting them from the supplier's product design — gives you more control over cost, flexibility and continuity.

    What to record

    If you're going through a planning system procurement or replacement, write down:

    • What your current planning product actually provides (not just case management — include everything bundled in).
    • Which of those capabilities your council also provides as corporate services.
    • For each capability: whether you intend it to come from the planning product, a corporate service, or a combination — and why.
    • What information needs to move between the planning system and each separated service.
    • Who will be responsible for making (and maintaining) each connection.
    • What must happen to data if you change supplier in future.
    • Which of your assumptions you have not yet tested.

    Recording these decisions explicitly helps you revisit them later — when a corporate service improves, a contract expires, or a new requirement emerges — without starting from scratch.
