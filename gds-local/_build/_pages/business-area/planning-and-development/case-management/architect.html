    Architecture and integration view

    Prototype — architect persona

    This version targets technical architects and solution designers.

    It assumes you already understand enterprise integration patterns. It aims to provide planning-specific detail you cannot easily derive from general architecture guidance. Confidence levels are marked inline — nothing here should be treated as validated without checking it against your own context.

    Premise

    Planning case management does not operate as a self-contained system. It depends on capabilities that most councils also provide for other services — payments, document storage, identity, notifications, geospatial data, records management. The architectural question is not whether these dependencies exist (they obviously do) but:

    • Where should the integration boundary sit for each capability?
    • What are the characteristics of each integration point that constrain your options?
    • What specifically about planning makes some of those boundaries harder than in other domains?
    • Where does current market structure create lock-in that a boundary decision could mitigate (or worsen)?

    If you already know all of this for your council, this page is not for you. If you're at the point of designing or evaluating a planning system's integration architecture, the intent is to surface the constraints and trade-offs that aren't obvious from supplier documentation.

    What makes planning technically different

    Every council domain has integration points with shared capabilities. Planning is architecturally distinctive in several ways that affect how you design those integrations: To validate

    Volume and shape of unstructured data

    A single planning application can generate hundreds of documents — architectural drawings, structural calculations, environmental reports, public representations, consultee responses. These are overwhelmingly unstructured (PDFs, TIFFs, large-format plans). The ratio of unstructured to structured data is far higher than in most council domains. This means:

    • Document management integration cannot be purely metadata-and-reference — the volume and file sizes create real storage, retrieval and indexing challenges.
    • A corporate DMS built for committee papers may not handle the scale or the access patterns (frequent retrieval during assessment, public register obligations).
    • Search and classification of planning documents requires domain awareness that generic enterprise search may not provide.

    Spatial data is not a lookup — it is structurally integral

    In most council services, geospatial information is a reference — you look up an address, retrieve a UPRN, and move on. In planning, spatial data is structurally embedded in the case logic:

    • The application boundary (red-line) is a legal artefact that defines the scope of the permission.
    • Determination requires intersection of that boundary with constraint layers (conservation areas, flood zones, TPOs, Article 4 directions).
    • Changes to the boundary during assessment can change which policies apply and which consultees must be notified.
    • Integration with GIS is not read-only — the planning system may need to write or amend spatial objects.

    This makes the geospatial integration tighter and more bidirectional than most domains require. A simple "query the corporate GIS for constraints" pattern may not be sufficient if the planning system also needs to store, display and allow amendment of application-specific geometries.

    Statutory timescales create hard coupling to notifications

    Planning has legally defined determination periods (typically 8 or 13 weeks). Missing these deadlines has direct consequences — the applicant gains a right of appeal. This means:

    • Notifications (acknowledgements, consultation triggers, decision notices) are not fire-and-forget. The planning system needs to know they were sent and when.
    • If notification delivery fails silently, the statutory clock may expire without proper consultation having occurred — creating legal risk.
    • Integration with notification services needs confirmation of delivery and timestamps, not just acceptance of a message into a queue.

    Public register obligations

    Planning authorities must maintain a public register of applications and decisions. This is not the same as "publishing data" — it is a statutory requirement with specific content and accessibility expectations. This affects:

    • Document management — documents placed on the register must be redacted for personal information but remain publicly accessible.
    • Records management — the register is a permanent, legally authoritative record, not a retention-managed archive.
    • Data ownership — the planning system, the register, and the DMS may all hold overlapping copies of "the same" document, with different redaction states.

    Market concentration and closed ecosystems

    The planning back-office market is dominated by a small number of suppliers whose products tend to provide integrated (bundled) document storage, GIS display, notification, and fee management within the planning application. This means:

    • The existing integration boundary for most councils is "everything inside the planning product" — whether or not that's architecturally desirable.
    • Extracting a capability (e.g. moving document storage to a corporate DMS) may require the supplier to expose APIs they have no commercial incentive to provide.
    • Replacing the planning system means migrating not just case data but all the bundled capability data (documents, spatial objects, payment records, audit trails).
    • The cost of migration from one supplier to another is widely reported to exceed £1M per authority, largely because of the data extraction complexity.

    Capability boundaries

    The question "should planning use the corporate capability or its own?" has a different answer for each integration point. The following is a framework for thinking through that boundary, not a prescription: To validate

    Capability boundary considerations
    Capability Arguments for using the corporate service Arguments for keeping it inside the planning product Key constraint
    Payments Fees are standard transactions; corporate payments provide reconciliation, PCI compliance and consistent citizen experience. GOV.UK Pay is free and available. Fee calculation logic is planning-specific (varies by development type, site area, CIL). The system that knows the application parameters is best placed to calculate the fee. Fee calculation vs. payment processing are separable. One can be internal while the other is shared.
    Documents Avoids duplicate storage costs; corporate DMS may offer better records management, retention and disposal. Volume, file size, access patterns and public register requirements may exceed corporate DMS design assumptions. Classification requires planning domain knowledge. If planning documents live in a corporate DMS, does the planning system need its own search and display layer on top? If so, is the separation adding value or just complexity?
    Identity Consistent citizen login across council services. Agents (planning professionals) who submit to multiple councils benefit from federated identity. Planning access control is case-specific (applicant sees their case, consultees see consultation documents, public sees the register). Role models are more complex than most council services. Authentication (who you are) is clearly a shared concern. Authorisation (what you can see on this case) is planning-specific. The boundary sits between them.
    Notifications Corporate notification services (GOV.UK Notify) provide reliable delivery, templates and audit. Consistency of communication across council services. Planning notifications are triggered by case-state transitions and have statutory timing requirements. The planning system must orchestrate when to notify, not just how. Orchestration (trigger logic) stays in planning. Delivery (sending the message) can be shared. The interface between them needs delivery confirmation and timestamps.
    Geospatial Corporate GIS holds authoritative constraint layers. Single source of truth for addresses and UPRNs. Planning needs to store, display and allow amendment of application-specific geometries (red-line boundaries). Intersection queries need sub-second response for interactive use. Read (constraints) vs. write (application boundaries) have different integration patterns. Reading from corporate GIS is straightforward; storing planning-specific spatial objects may need to be local.
    Records management Corporate records management applies consistent retention, disposal and accessibility rules. Avoids planning maintaining its own policy. The planning register is permanent and public — different from retention-managed corporate records. The "record" is a composite (case + documents + decisions + spatial objects + consultations) not a single file. The planning register may not fit corporate records management assumptions about lifecycle. It needs permanent, publicly accessible, composite records — which is unusual.

    Integration characteristics

    If you decide to separate a capability, the integration needs to support specific characteristics. These are not generic "use an API" statements — they describe what the interface must actually do in a planning context: Provisional

    Payments → Planning case management

    Pattern: Synchronous callback or webhook. Payment service processes transaction and returns status + reference to the planning system. Must support: Reliable correlation (case reference → payment reference), idempotent status updates (network failures must not create duplicate payments or orphaned cases), confirmation of amount (planning calculates, payment confirms). Failure case: Payment succeeds but callback fails — case appears unpaid. Needs reconciliation mechanism.

    Geospatial → Planning case management

    Pattern: Synchronous query for constraint intersection (sub-second for interactive use), plus write-back for application boundaries. Must support: Intersection query (polygon vs. constraint layers), UPRN/address resolution, storage and retrieval of application-specific geometries (GeoJSON or equivalent). Failure case: Constraint layers updated in GIS after application submitted — does planning re-evaluate, or is it point-in-time? Both are defensible but must be explicit.

    Planning case management → Notifications

    Pattern: Event-driven. Planning emits notification request on case-state transition; notification service delivers and confirms. Must support: Delivery confirmation with timestamp (not just acceptance into queue), template selection (planning specifies type; notification service resolves template), multiple channels (email, letter, SMS) per notification type. Failure case: Delivery fails or is delayed past statutory deadline. Planning system must detect this and alert — cannot silently assume delivery.

    Document management ↔ Planning case management

    Pattern: Bidirectional. Planning writes documents (or references) to DMS at submission and during case lifecycle; reads them back for assessment and public register. Must support: Large file upload (architectural drawings can be 50MB+), case-linked retrieval (all documents for case X), classification metadata (document type, sensitivity, public-register eligibility), redaction state tracking. Failure case: Document stored in DMS but reference not written back to planning system — document exists but is invisible to the case. Needs transactional consistency or reconciliation.

    Identity → Planning case management

    Pattern: Federated authentication (OIDC/OAuth 2.0) with planning-specific authorisation rules applied locally. Must support: Multiple user types (applicant, agent, officer, consultee, public), case-specific access (applicant sees their case, not all cases), role transitions (applicant becomes appellant on the same case). Failure case: Authorisation rules are too coarse — either over-sharing (consultee sees applicant's financial information) or under-sharing (officer cannot see consultation responses). Case-specific ABAC is harder to get right than role-based access.

    Failure modes and lock-in

    Architectural decisions about capability boundaries should be assessed against how they fail, not just how they work: To validate

    If everything is inside the planning product

    • Lock-in intensifies: Replacing the product means migrating documents, spatial data, payment history, notification logs and case records simultaneously. Cost and risk are maximised.
    • Duplication of corporate investment: Payment processing, DMS, notification infrastructure duplicated inside planning — but only available to planning.
    • Single point of failure: If the planning product is unavailable, all dependent capabilities are also unavailable. No graceful degradation.
    • Innovation constraint: Improving any single capability (e.g. better GIS) requires the planning supplier to implement it. Your roadmap is their roadmap.

    If everything is separated

    • Integration complexity: Every capability boundary is an interface to build, test, monitor and maintain. Total cost of integration may exceed cost of duplication.
    • Operational fragility: Planning case management becomes dependent on N external services. If any is slow or unavailable, planning throughput is affected. Statutory deadlines don't pause for service outages.
    • Accountability diffusion: When a case fails (e.g. notification not sent, payment not recorded), whose problem is it? Distributed ownership can mean distributed blame and slow resolution.
    • Capability mismatch: Corporate services optimised for other domains may not meet planning's specific needs (volume, access patterns, statutory requirements) — forcing workarounds that negate the reuse benefit.

    Neither extreme is inherently correct. The useful architectural work is identifying — for each capability — which failure mode is more costly and more likely in your council's specific context.

    Data ownership

    For each integration point, you need to establish which system is authoritative. This is harder than it sounds because planning creates composite records: Provisional

    Information Candidate system of record Complication
    Application details (what was proposed) Planning case management If submitted through a separate portal (e.g. PlanX), where does the authoritative record live — the submission system or the back office?
    Site boundary (red-line) Planning case management (application-specific geometry) Corporate GIS holds the base map and constraints. Planning holds the application-specific overlay. Both are "spatial data" but have different lifecycles.
    Constraint data (conservation areas, flood zones) Corporate GIS / national datasets Planning needs a point-in-time snapshot of constraints at submission (for legal defensibility). Does it copy them or reference them? If referenced, what if the source changes?
    Payment record Corporate payments / finance system Planning needs the payment reference and confirmation. Finance needs the reconciliation. Both need to agree the case was paid — but they're recording different things about the same event.
    Submitted documents DMS or planning system (depends on architecture) If in a DMS: planning holds references. If planning product holds them: how are they governed post-case-closure? Who applies retention/disposal?
    Decision and reasons Planning case management The decision notice is also a records management artefact and a public register entry. Three concerns, one document — which system is authoritative for each purpose?

    Worked example: payments

    This walks through the boundary decision for a single capability to illustrate how the framework above applies in practice.

    The decomposition

    "Payment capability" conflates at least three distinct functions:

    1. Fee calculation — determining the amount due based on application type, site area, CIL liability, exemptions.
    2. Payment processing — taking the money (card transaction, bank transfer), PCI compliance, receipting.
    3. Reconciliation — matching the payment to the case, recording it in the finance system, handling refunds.

    These three functions do not need to live in the same system. A useful boundary might be:

    • Fee calculation stays in the planning system (it requires application-specific knowledge — development type, floor area, CIL zones).
    • Payment processing uses the corporate payment service (GOV.UK Pay or equivalent — PCI compliance is their problem, not yours).
    • Reconciliation needs an interface between the planning system (case reference, expected amount) and the finance system (actual payment received, GL coding).

    Interface requirements

    For this boundary to work, the integration must handle:

    • Planning sends: case reference, calculated fee amount, return URL.
    • Payment service returns: payment reference, status (success/failure/pending), timestamp.
    • Failure path: if callback fails, planning must poll or the payment service must retry. An unrecorded successful payment must never block case progression indefinitely.
    • Refund path: who initiates (planning officer or finance?) and how is the planning case updated?

    What this example demonstrates

    The useful architectural decision isn't "use corporate payments: yes/no" — it's identifying that the capability contains separable functions with different natural homes. Fee calculation is domain-specific. Payment processing is a commodity. Reconciliation is a shared concern. Decomposing at that level produces a clearer, more defensible boundary than "all in" or "all out".

    Open questions

    This page does not yet resolve the following. If you have direct experience that addresses any of these, that is the most valuable feedback you could provide:

    • In councils that have separated document storage from the planning product, what operational problems emerged? (Specifically: access patterns, search quality, large-file performance, public register workflows.)
    • What does the actual API surface of current planning products look like? (Are suppliers exposing integration points, or is integration restricted to import/export and RPA?)
    • Where councils have used GOV.UK Pay for planning fees, how is the fee calculation communicated to the payment service? Is there a standard interface or does each council build bespoke?
    • What happens to spatial data (application boundaries, officer annotations) when a council migrates between planning systems? Is it exported, or effectively lost?
    • Do any councils operate planning case management with genuinely separated capabilities (corporate DMS, federated identity, shared payments) — and if so, what does the integration architecture actually look like in practice?
    • Are there councils where the cost of maintaining integrations to corporate services exceeded the cost that bundled equivalents would have incurred?
